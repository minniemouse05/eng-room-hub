# Data: Practice, Quiz, Final Exam

An AI is only as good as its practice problems—and how you quiz it before the real test matters more than you'd think.

## Why Data Is Everything

Here's a truth that took the AI field decades to fully appreciate: the algorithm matters less than the data.

A simple algorithm with great data usually beats a fancy algorithm with bad data.

<Callout type="key" title="The ingredients metaphor">
Data is your ingredient list. Garbage ingredients, garbage meal—no matter how talented the chef or how expensive the kitchen.
</Callout>

When someone pitches you an AI solution, your first question shouldn't be "what algorithm does it use?" It should be "what data was it trained on?"

---

## The Three Data Splits

When training an AI, we split data into three buckets. Each serves a different purpose—and skipping any of them causes problems.

### Training Data = Practice Problems

This is what the AI learns from. It sees thousands of examples—emails labeled spam/not-spam, houses with their sale prices—and figures out the patterns.

<Callout type="tip" title="If this feels fuzzy">
Imagine studying for a test by doing practice problems. The more varied and realistic your practice, the better you'll do on the real thing.
</Callout>

**What happens with too little training data?** The AI doesn't see enough examples to learn reliable patterns. It's like studying from only 5 practice problems before a big exam.

---

### Validation Data = Pop Quizzes

While training, we periodically test the AI on data it hasn't seen yet. This helps us tune the recipe—adjust settings, catch problems early.

**Why it matters:** Without validation, an AI might just memorize the training data instead of learning general patterns.

<Callout type="warning" title="Overfitting: when memorization beats understanding">
"Overfitting" is when an AI memorizes specific training examples instead of learning transferable patterns. It's like a student who memorizes answers word-for-word but can't handle a rephrased question.

Signs of overfitting: Training accuracy is 98%, but validation accuracy is only 65%.
</Callout>

---

### Test Data = The Final Exam

Once training is done, we evaluate on completely fresh data. This tells us how the AI will actually perform in the real world.

**The golden rule:** Never peek at test data during training. That's cheating—and it gives you false confidence about how well your AI will perform.

## When Ingredients Go Bad: Bias

Here's the uncomfortable truth: if your training data contains biases, your AI will learn those biases. And often amplify them.

<Callout type="example" title="A concrete example">
A hiring algorithm trained on a company's historical hiring data. If that company historically hired fewer women for technical roles, the AI learns "women = less likely to be good hires."

The AI isn't malicious. It's just pattern-matching on biased ingredients.
</Callout>

### Where Bias Hides

Bias isn't always obvious. It can lurk in:

| Hidden in... | Example |
|---|---|
| **Who's included** | Training data from only wealthy neighborhoods |
| **Who's excluded** | Medical data that underrepresents certain demographics |
| **How labels were assigned** | "Successful employee" defined by managers with their own biases |
| **What features you chose** | Using zip code as a proxy for things correlated with race |
| **Historical inequities** | Loan approval data from decades of discriminatory lending |

<Callout type="pitfall" title="More data isn't always better">
10,000 biased examples will produce a more confidently biased AI than 1,000 biased examples. Volume doesn't fix representativeness problems.
</Callout>

## Common Misconceptions

<Callout type="pitfall" title="'The AI decided' is misleading">
The AI learned from human-generated data, which reflects human decisions and biases. The AI is downstream of those choices. When an AI produces a biased outcome, the root cause is usually the training data.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="'We just need more data'">
More data helps only if it's more *representative* data. Collecting 10x more data from the same biased source just gives you a more confident biased model.
</Callout>

## Test Yourself

<Callout type="checklist" title="Mini-check">
**Question:** You're building an AI to recommend job candidates. Your training data comes from the past 10 years of hiring decisions at a company with known diversity problems. What's the risk?

**Answer:** The AI will likely learn and replicate those diversity problems—recommending candidates who match the biased historical pattern, even if that pattern was unfair.
</Callout>
