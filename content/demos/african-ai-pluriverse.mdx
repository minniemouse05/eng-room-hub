# An African AI Pluriverse

Most AI development happens in a handful of wealthy countries, reflecting their values, languages, and priorities. But AI doesn't have to be one-size-fits-all. This lesson explores an alternative vision—AI that is socially just, community-centered, transformative, and rooted in local knowledge. Drawing from research on AI in Africa, we'll examine what AI could look like when designed by and for diverse communities.

---

## What Is the "Pluriverse"?

The concept of a "pluriverse" challenges the idea that there's one right way to do things. Instead of a single "universe" of AI development (dominated by Silicon Valley), a pluriverse imagines:

- **Multiple valid approaches** to technology
- **Diverse value systems** shaping design
- **Local knowledge** informing solutions
- **Community needs** driving development

> "A world where many worlds fit." — Zapatista saying often used in pluriversal thinking

Applied to AI, this means questioning assumptions about what AI should be, who should build it, and whose problems it should solve.

---

## The Current AI Landscape in Africa

To understand alternative visions, we first need to see the current reality:

### Opportunities

- **Growing tech ecosystems** in hubs like Lagos, Nairobi, and Cape Town
- **Unique challenges** that AI could help address (healthcare access, agriculture, education)
- **Young, tech-savvy populations** ready to innovate
- **Mobile-first** digital infrastructure enabling new approaches

### Challenges

- **Language gaps:** Most AI works poorly in African languages
- **Data colonialism:** Data extracted from Africa often benefits foreign companies
- **Infrastructure:** Unreliable power and connectivity limit AI adoption
- **Brain drain:** Skilled AI practitioners often leave for opportunities elsewhere
- **Colonial legacies:** Power imbalances shape who builds AI and for whom

## Key Traits of a Just AI Future

Research into African perspectives on AI reveals several key traits that should characterize AI systems:

### 1. Socially Just

AI should reduce inequalities, not deepen them.

**What this means:**
- AI shouldn't reinforce existing discrimination
- Benefits should reach marginalized communities
- Development should include diverse voices
- Harm to vulnerable groups should be actively prevented

**Questions to ask:**
- Who benefits from this AI system?
- Who might be harmed or excluded?
- Does this AI challenge or reinforce power imbalances?

### 2. Community-Centered

AI should serve communities, not extract from them.

**What this means:**
- Communities should be involved in AI design
- Local needs should drive development priorities
- Data should benefit the people it comes from
- Solutions should be accountable to those they affect

**Questions to ask:**
- Was this AI designed with community input?
- Do affected communities have ongoing voice?
- Who controls the data and the system?

### 3. Transformative

AI should help change unjust systems, not just make them more efficient.

**What this means:**
- AI can challenge harmful structures, not just automate them
- Technology should expand human capabilities and freedoms
- Innovation should serve liberation, not just profit
- The goal is better systems, not just faster ones

**Questions to ask:**
- Does this AI help transform unjust conditions?
- Or does it just make existing problems more efficient?
- What kind of future does this AI build toward?

### 4. Rooted in Local Knowledge

AI should build on—not erase—existing knowledge systems.

**What this means:**
- Indigenous and local knowledge has value
- AI shouldn't require abandoning cultural practices
- Multiple ways of knowing should be respected
- Technology should complement, not replace, traditional wisdom

**Questions to ask:**
- Does this AI respect local knowledge systems?
- Are local languages and contexts supported?
- Does the AI work with or against existing practices?

## Practical Implications for CSOs

What does this mean for civil society organizations considering AI?

### Procurement and Partnerships

When evaluating AI tools or vendors:

- **Ask about diversity:** Who built this? Were diverse perspectives included?
- **Check language support:** Does it work in languages your communities use?
- **Understand data practices:** Where does data go? Who benefits?
- **Look for accountability:** Can you hold the provider accountable if things go wrong?

### Design and Implementation

When developing AI solutions:

- **Center affected communities:** Involve them from the start
- **Build on local knowledge:** What existing practices can AI enhance?
- **Plan for equity:** Actively work to ensure marginalized groups benefit
- **Enable local control:** Can communities shape how AI is used?

### Advocacy and Policy

When engaging on AI policy:

- **Amplify diverse voices:** Whose perspectives are missing from AI governance?
- **Challenge data colonialism:** Advocate for data sovereignty
- **Support local innovation:** Push for investment in diverse AI ecosystems
- **Question dominant narratives:** Challenge assumptions about what AI should be

## Examples of Alternative Approaches

Around the world, communities are building AI differently:

### Language Technology

Projects working to bring AI capabilities to underrepresented languages—not by waiting for big tech, but through community-driven efforts that prioritize local needs.

### Agricultural AI

Farmers combining traditional knowledge with AI tools, where technology augments rather than replaces generations of agricultural wisdom.

### Health Information

AI systems designed with community input to provide health information in culturally appropriate ways, respecting local practices alongside medical science.

### Creative Expression

Artists using AI tools to express and preserve cultural heritage, rather than having AI homogenize creative output toward Western norms.

## The Bigger Vision

A pluriversal approach to AI isn't just about Africa—it's about imagining AI that works for all of humanity:

> **Current paradigm:** A few companies build AI, everyone else uses it.
>
> **Pluriversal alternative:** Many communities shape AI to serve their diverse needs.

This vision requires:
- **Redistribution of power** in who builds and governs AI
- **Respect for different values** in what AI should do
- **Investment in diversity** in who participates in AI development
- **Accountability structures** that serve all communities

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **One-size-fits-all AI serves some and harms others** - Dominant AI reflects dominant values
2. **Socially just AI actively reduces inequality** - Not neutral, but intentionally equitable
3. **Community-centered AI puts people in control** - Built with, not just for, communities
4. **Transformative AI changes systems** - Not just efficiency, but justice
5. **Local knowledge matters** - AI should enhance, not erase, diverse ways of knowing
