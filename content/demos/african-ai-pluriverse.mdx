# What If AI Wasn't One-Size-Fits-All?

Most AI is built in a handful of wealthy countries, by a small group of companies, reflecting a narrow set of values and languages.

**But it doesn't have to be this way.**

Research from Africa and other regions imagines a different future—a "pluriverse" where many different approaches to AI can coexist, each shaped by the communities it serves.

This lesson explores what that looks like in practice—and how CSOs can apply these principles even when using AI built elsewhere.

## The Pluriverse Idea

<Callout type="key" title="One sentence version">
Instead of one dominant way of building AI, imagine many different approaches—each shaped by different communities, values, and knowledge systems.
</Callout>

The word "pluriverse" comes from the Zapatista saying: *"A world where many worlds fit."*

Applied to AI, it challenges assumptions like:

- AI must be built by tech giants
- English-first is good enough
- Silicon Valley values are universal
- Communities should adapt to AI (not the other way around)

### Why this matters for CSOs

Even if you're not building AI yourself, these ideas help you:

- **Evaluate AI tools** — Does this tool respect diverse contexts?
- **Push back on vendors** — What communities shaped this product?
- **Design better** — How can we center the people we serve?
- **Advocate** — What should AI governance look like globally?

## Four Principles for Just AI

Research into African perspectives on AI reveals four key traits that should characterize AI systems—wherever they're built.

### 1. Socially Just

**The idea:** AI should reduce inequality, not deepen it.

<Callout type="example" title="What this looks like">
**Unjust:** A loan-approval AI trained on biased historical data denies credit to certain neighborhoods—perpetuating the same discrimination that existed before AI.

**Just:** The same AI is audited for bias, tested with affected communities, and designed with safeguards that actively work to counteract historical patterns.
</Callout>

**Questions to ask:**

- Who benefits from this AI? Who's excluded?
- Does this AI challenge power imbalances—or reinforce them?
- Are the people most affected by this AI involved in its design?

### 2. Community-Centered

**The idea:** AI should serve communities, not extract from them.

**Questions to ask:**

- Was this AI designed with meaningful community input?
- Who controls the data? Who benefits from it?
- Can affected communities influence how this AI is used?

### 3. Transformative

**The idea:** AI should help change unjust systems—not just make them more efficient.

<Callout type="warning" title="The efficiency trap">
It's easy to use AI to make existing processes faster. But if those processes were unjust to begin with, you've just automated injustice.

*Faster* isn't the goal. *Better* is.
</Callout>

**Examples of the difference:**

| Efficient (but not transformative) | Transformative |
|---|---|
| AI speeds up visa rejections | AI identifies patterns of discrimination in visa decisions |
| AI automates denial letters | AI helps applicants understand their rights |
| AI predicts who will "fail" | AI identifies what support people need to succeed |

**Questions to ask:**

- Does this AI help transform unjust conditions—or just automate them?
- What kind of future does this AI build toward?
- Could this AI be used to challenge power, not just serve it?

### 4. Rooted in Local Knowledge

**The idea:** AI should build on—not erase—existing knowledge systems.

<Callout type="example" title="Real examples">
**Language:** Instead of waiting for big tech to support African languages, community-led projects are building language AI *with* local speakers, incorporating local contexts and idioms.

**Agriculture:** AI tools that work *alongside* traditional farming knowledge—treating generations of expertise as valuable data, not obstacles to overcome.

**Health:** Systems designed to complement traditional medicine practices, not dismiss them as invalid.
</Callout>

**Questions to ask:**

- Does this AI respect local knowledge systems?
- Are local languages and contexts genuinely supported—or just translated?
- Does the AI work with existing practices—or against them?

## Questions for Your Work

Reflect on these for your own context:

<Callout type="checklist" title="Pluriverse self-assessment">

**Socially Just**

- [ ] Do we know who benefits and who's excluded from the AI we use?
- [ ] Have we assessed whether our AI tools reinforce or challenge inequalities?

**Community-Centered**

- [ ] Have affected communities shaped how we use AI?
- [ ] Do we have meaningful feedback mechanisms?

**Transformative**

- [ ] Are we using AI to change unjust systems—or just speed them up?
- [ ] What future are we building toward with these tools?

**Rooted in Local Knowledge**

- [ ] Does our AI work with local languages and contexts?
- [ ] Do we treat community knowledge as valuable input?

</Callout>

## Common Pitfalls

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Treating 'community input' as a checkbox">
Showing a community your finished AI and asking "What do you think?" isn't meaningful participation. Communities should shape *design*, not just *review*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Assuming Western AI + translation = good enough">
AI built on English-language, Western data doesn't become culturally appropriate just because you translate the interface. The assumptions are baked in deeper.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Waiting for perfect alternatives">
You might not have access to community-built, locally-rooted AI tools today. That doesn't mean you can't apply these principles to how you use and advocate around the tools that do exist.
</Callout>
