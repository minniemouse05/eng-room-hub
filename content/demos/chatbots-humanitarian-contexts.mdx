# Should Your Organization Use a Chatbot?

A refugee arrives at a border crossing at 2am. They need to know: *Where can I get medical help? Is my family's asylum claim still processing? What documents do I need?*

A chatbot could answer instantly, in their language, any time of day. Or it could give wrong information that puts them in danger.

**This lesson helps you figure out which outcome is more likely for your context.**

A chatbot is software that talks with people through text or voice. Think of it as a spectrum:

**Why CSOs consider chatbots:**

- **Reach more people.** Your 3-person team can't answer 10,000 questions a day. A bot can.
- **Be available 24/7.** Crises don't keep office hours.
- **Speak multiple languages.** Without hiring 20 translators.
- **Reduce stigma.** Some people share sensitive information more easily with a machine.

<Callout type="key">
Chatbots are tools for *scaling information delivery*. They're not tools for building relationships, making complex judgments, or replacing human expertise.
</Callout>

## The Real Risks

Here's what can go wrong—and does go wrong—when chatbots meet vulnerable populations:

### Wrong information, delivered confidently

<Callout type="warning" title="This happens more than you'd think">
A health chatbot tells someone their symptoms aren't serious. They delay seeking care. The chatbot was wrong.

Unlike a human, chatbots don't say "I'm not sure" or "let me check." They just answer. Even when they shouldn't.
</Callout>

**Ask yourself:** If our chatbot gives wrong information about [medical care / legal rights / safety procedures], what's the worst realistic outcome?

### Cultural context gets lost

Most AI is trained on English-language, Western data. It often:

- Misunderstands local dialects and expressions
- Misses cultural cues about distress or urgency
- Applies inappropriate assumptions about gender, family, or social norms

<Callout type="example" title="Real situation">
A mental health chatbot trained in the US missed that a user saying "I want to sleep forever" was expressing suicidal thoughts—because that phrasing wasn't in its training data. The chatbot responded with sleep hygiene tips.
</Callout>

### Data becomes a liability

Every conversation with your chatbot is data. Data that could:

- Be subpoenaed by governments
- Be leaked in a breach
- Expose sensitive patterns (who's asking about asylum, when, from where)
- Be used to train commercial AI systems (check your vendor's terms!)

### The human connection disappears

For someone in crisis, sometimes what helps most is *being heard by another person*. A chatbot can't provide that. And if people *think* they're talking to a human when they're not, trust erodes.

### You exclude the people who need you most

Chatbots assume:

- A smartphone or computer
- Reliable internet
- Ability to read and type
- Comfort with technology

The most marginalized people often have none of these.

## Common Pitfalls

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Building first, asking questions later">
Many organizations build chatbots because they seem innovative, then discover the tool doesn't match the need. Always start with the problem, not the solution.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Hiding the 'talk to a human' option">
If users can't easily reach a real person, they'll either give up or trust the bot when they shouldn't. Make human escalation prominent and easy.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Assuming the vendor handles ethics">
Your chatbot vendor's terms of service probably let them use conversations to train their AI. They probably store data in jurisdictions you haven't considered. Read the fine print.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Measuring the wrong things">
"10,000 conversations!" sounds impressive. But: Did people get accurate information? Did they find human help when needed? Did anyone get hurt? Those metrics are harder to track—and more important.
</Callout>
